Getting the first sync finished

More than half of prospects who started Fivetran's onboarding never completed a data sync. I was the first designer on the app, and this is the flow we rebuilt.

The problem

Fivetran's managed connectors handled schema normalization and table structure, so data teams didn't have to. But getting a pipeline running still required going through IT or a data engineer. That was costly, slow, and squarely in Fivetran's way.

The brief was a system that let non-technical users self-serve. The measurable version of it was narrower: get more people through setup to a finished first sync.

Source and destination setup No credentials for source systems. Anxiety about connecting the warehouse first. Technical setup forms people couldn't finish. And no sense of what to do next.
Schema setup Long waits fetching large database schemas, no idea which objects to sync, and real fear that PII would go with them.
After setup Notification email too noisy to act on, sync errors hard to monitor, and almost no visibility into progress on large historical syncs.

Synthesized from customer interviews across key personas.

Phase 1

Reorder the steps, and put the documentation inside the step that needs it

I mapped the existing flow in low-fidelity first, marking every pain point so none of them got quietly lost in the redesign. Then we changed the order of steps, standardized the visual system, and rebuilt the layout to make room for embedded docs. Education in place, at the moment someone is stuck on a technical form.

My PM and I agreed up front to measure it with the metrics our analytics team already tracked: time to value, and dropoff at each key step. No new instrumentation, no argument later about whether it worked.

We added a Welcome step and made things worse

The embedded docs did what we hoped: more completed syncs during onboarding. The new Welcome step did not. Asking people to choose a source and a destination before anything else created more dropoff earlier in the flow than the version we replaced.

First release: the Welcome step

We shipped a follow-up quickly and made that first choice non-committal. You could move on without deciding. Sync rates at onboarding improved 36% overall after that release, and we kept tuning individual steps against ongoing usage tracking.

First release: full onboarding flow
Phase 2

Treat PII anxiety as a design problem, not a documentation problem

The most emotional thing in the interviews was not knowing whether personal data was about to be copied into the warehouse. People hesitated at the schema step, and hesitation at the schema step means no first sync.

Phase 2 gave users an explicit review of the schema and its PII risks before syncing. We stated the goal as reducing PII anxiety and raising sync rates, in those words, so the team knew what we were buying with the extra step.

Research turned up problems across the entire journey and we could not take them all on. Enabling self-service meant starting where confidence broke down, which was setup. The rest phased in behind it.

Redesigning the core dashboardBefore and after

Setup gets people to their first sync; the dashboard is where they spend the rest of their time. I redesigned the two pages at its core, the connector list and the connector detail page, around the questions operators actually ask: where is my connector, and is it healthy?

Connector list

Before
After
  • Filtering. Search by name plus status and source filters, so a list of hundreds of connectors narrows to the one you need in a couple of steps.
  • No more nesting. Connectors used to be grouped under their source, like “Amplitude (2)”, which hid them behind an extra click. Each connector is now its own row.
  • Organized metadata. Name, source type, status and last sync sit in consistent, sortable columns instead of being stacked under each name.

Connector detail

Before
After
  • A legible sync chart. The wall of per-minute ticks became one clear bar per sync for each stage, with warnings in their own color, a “now” marker and timing on hover.
  • Key information up front. Sync frequency, average duration, schema changes, last successful sync and open warnings sit in a single summary strip at the top.
  • Built for monitoring. Alerts, user actions and stats sit side by side under the chart, so what went wrong, who changed what and how the connector is performing are visible in one view.
Blueprint design systemBuilt in parallel with the UX work

I didn’t treat the design system as a separate project. While the onboarding work moved through its phases, I built Blueprint alongside it: whenever a pattern repeated across screens, it became a token or a component, with every state documented, and went straight back into the next round of design.

Engineering adopted Blueprint as the shared foundation for the product UI. It wasn’t a deliverable handed over at the end; it was an outcome of the design process itself.

What I took away

Beyond setup I worked across the roadmap: transformations, the sync chart UI, and more. The lesson that stuck was about words.

Coming into data integration new, I had to learn the domain properly before I could write a single useful label, and I had to pull the right stakeholders into copy decisions rather than making them alone. You cannot design an educational experience for technical and non-technical users at the same time until you actually understand the thing being explained.
